01
Dataset overview
🌌🛸

This dataset tracks active satellites currently in Earth orbit, compiled by the Union of Concerned Scientists. Each row is one satellite — who operates it, what it's used for, and the orbital mechanics that keep it up there.

I picked the UCS Satellite Database because it gives a real, current snapshot of every operational satellite in orbit — not a historical or simulated dataset. It sits at the intersection of my interest in aerospace and the kind of structured, numeric data that's genuinely useful for statistical analysis. With over 7,000 satellites tracked, it captures the full diversity of the industry: national space agencies, private companies, military programs, and university projects all in one table, each with hard orbital mechanics numbers (mass, altitude, period) alongside the human/organizational side (who owns it, what it's for). That mix of categorical and numerical detail is exactly why it works well for this activity.

03
Variable identification
🔭🧮
🌍
🛰️
Categorical
  • Country of Operator — nominal, no inherent order
  • Purpose — nominal, describes mission type
  • Users — nominal, ownership sector
🪐
⭐
Numerical
  • Perigee (km) — continuous, ratio scale
  • Apogee (km) — continuous, ratio scale
  • Inclination (°) — continuous, interval/ratio
  • Launch mass (kg) — continuous, ratio scale
🌍
Categorical

Country of Operator, Purpose, and Users are categorical because their values are labels, not quantities — "Purpose" being "Communications" or "Earth Observation" can't be measured or ranked numerically. All three sit on the nominal scale, since there's no natural order between categories (e.g. "Military" isn't inherently higher or lower than "Commercial").

🪐
Numerical

Perigee, Apogee, Inclination, and Launch Mass are numerical because they represent measurable quantities from real instruments. All four are ratio scale — they have a true zero (0 km altitude or 0 kg mass genuinely means none), and ratios between values are meaningful (twice the mass really is twice as heavy). All four are also continuous, since they can take any value within a range (e.g. 51.6° or 51.63°), not just whole-number counts.

04
Scales of measurement
📏🧭

There are four scales of measurement — nominal, ordinal, interval, and ratio. Every variable in this dataset falls into either the nominal or the ratio scale.

🧭
🌍
Nominal scale
  • Country of Operator — a label with no order or rank
  • Purpose — a category name, not a quantity
  • Users — an ownership-sector label
📏
🛰️
Ratio scale
  • Perigee (km) — true zero, ratios are meaningful
  • Apogee (km) — true zero, ratios are meaningful
  • Inclination (°) — 0° is a genuine absence of tilt
  • Launch mass (kg) — 0 kg genuinely means no mass
🧭
Nominal

Country of Operator, Purpose, and Users only name or label a group — there's no meaningful order (e.g. "Commercial" isn't higher or lower than "Military") and no arithmetic can be done on them. That's the defining feature of a nominal scale.

📏
Ratio

Perigee, Apogee, Inclination, and Launch Mass are all measured on a ratio scale because each has a true, non-arbitrary zero point (0 km, 0°, 0 kg all mean "none of this quantity"), and ratios between values are meaningful — a satellite with an 800 kg launch mass really is twice as heavy as one at 400 kg. This is what separates ratio from interval data, where zero is arbitrary (like 0°C) and ratios don't hold.

🚫
Ordinal & interval

None of the variables I picked are ordinal (ranked categories like "Low / Medium / High") or interval (equal spacing but no true zero, like temperature in Celsius) — the dataset simply doesn't have that kind of column, since it mixes plain organizational labels with hard physical measurements.

05
Discrete & continuous variables
🔢📈

Only numerical variables can be split into discrete or continuous — categorical variables don't apply here since they're not counted or measured on a number line.

📈
🪐
Continuous
  • Perigee (km) — can take any decimal value
  • Apogee (km) — can take any decimal value
  • Inclination (°) — measured to fractions of a degree
  • Launch mass (kg) — measured to fractions of a kilogram
🔢
🛰️
Discrete
  • Not present in this dataset
  • e.g. a hypothetical "Number of payloads" column would be
📈
Continuous

Perigee, Apogee, Inclination, and Launch Mass are all continuous because they come from physical measurement, not counting — they can take literally any value within a range (a satellite can sit at an inclination of 51.6°, 51.63°, or 51.637°), limited only by how precisely the instrument records it.

🔢
Discrete

None of the four numerical variables I picked are discrete, since discrete data only comes from counting whole, separate units, which can only take whole-number values with no in-between. Looking at the full UCS database (68 columns, including Eccentricity, Dry Mass, Power, and Expected Lifetime), every single numerical field is a measured quantity rather than a count — there's no "number of X" style column anywhere in the source data, so a discrete variable simply isn't available to select from this dataset. I've kept a hypothetical example alongside for contrast, to show what discrete data would look like if it were present.

Earth Observation and Communications dominate the dataset, together accounting for the majority of satellites, followed by Navigation/Global Positioning and Technology Development — with everything else contributing only a small fraction each.

Why this chart

A scatter plot shows the relationship between two continuous variables — here, whether a satellite's perigee predicts its apogee.

Satellites cluster into three distinct bands (LEO ~300–900km, MEO ~19,000–23,500km, GEO ~35,700km) rather than a smooth spread, reflecting the different orbital regimes used for different purposes.
